I have been trying to generate musical-sounding rhythms and melodies using Galois Fields.
[[https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf][Euclidean (algo)Rhythms]] are frequently used in electronic music, so there is some prior art for mathy things producing musically interesting results.
Euclidean Rhythms provide ways to "fill space optimally," which ends up sounding musical.
In $GF(2^n)$, Galois Field elements are bitstrings, which map naturally to gate sequences.
Since these sequences are field elements that we can do math over, we can manipulate them to form new sequences, combine them in exciting ways, or move between them.
It's likely that there's some way to produce something musical.

Most of what I've tried so far hasn't been interesting, but one attempt produced a series of repeated (decent sounding) shifts of the same sequence, which I found surprising.
We need two things to understand this: a way to write rhythms as zeros and ones, and a way to treat those same zeros and ones as elements of a Galois Field.

* Ingredients
First we'll need to go over a few prerequisites.

** Gates and Sequences
In electronic music, sequences of "gates" express rhythms.
A gate is either "open" or "closed."
An "open" gate says "play this instrument at this step" and a "closed" gate says "play nothing at this step."
A gate sequence is a bunch of gates in a row, like "open closed open open", which would mean the associated sound is played, skipped, played, played (repeating forever).

If we use 1 for "open" and 0 for "closed," we can write sequences compactly.
For example, we can write a kick (bass drum) pattern of =1000=, a snare pattern of =0010=, and a hihat pattern of =0101=.
Read these one digit at a time, left to right: =1000= is "one zero zero zero", not "one thousand".
Drawn out, those would be:

** Galois Fields
I'll attempt to explain Galois Fields well enough here to follow the rest of the post, but I recommend reading [[https://xorvoid.com/galois_fields_for_great_good_00.html][Learn you Galois Fields for Great Good]] if you'd actually like to learn how they work.

When people talk about math, they are typically discussing numbers and operations.
The most familiar operations are addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
These operations take (usually) two numbers and give you back a new one.

We've come up with a bunch of rules that these operations follow.
One such rule is the "distributive property," which says: =x × (y + z) = x × y + x × z=.

Sometimes we change the way the operations work.
When doing "clock math" (math with time), we say all numbers greater than 12 "wrap around", so =6+7 = 1= (=13 - 12 = 1=).
Math (for some definition of math) still works on a clock face.

If we can change what operations do, can we also change what numbers /are/?
Maybe; we could, for example, replace numbers with drum patterns, then define specific operations over these patterns that allow us to "add patterns together."
Imagine "adding" two patterns together with a simple rule:
#+begin_quote
In each output position, the gate is open (1) only when the gate in the same position was open in /exactly/ one of the two input patterns.
#+end_quote

Then, for a full sequence, we'd say: =0110 + 1101 = 1011= (check this yourself).

We've changed our numbers and changed our operations.
Are we still doing math?
We have a new "add" operation, does it follow any familiar rules?
In normal math, multiplication is "adding a value to itself N times", where =N= is a number.
Can we even define multiplication at all now when =N= is a gate sequence?

It turns out that the answer to these questions is /sometimes/.
For /some/ combinations of "things" and "operations," the familiar rules of math keep working (the distributive property, for example).
When a combination allows the rules to work, we call it a /field/.
A field built from only a /finite/ collection of things is called a Finite Field, or Galois Field (named after [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89variste_Galois][Évariste Galois]]).

*** Prime Number Clock Math
On a clock, "wrapping around" is secretly a remainder: =13 ÷ 12= is "1 with a remainder of 1."
The remainder is what the clock shows.
We call the wrap-around number the "modulus"; this style of math is called "modular arithmetic".

We can now build a working Galois Field using:
- Numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 (7 total)
- Operations: Clock-math/modular arithmetic

There are 7 total numbers, so 7 is our modulus.
This means, for example, =4 + 6 = 3 (mod 7)=, where the =(mod 7)= is notation reminding us that the modulus is 7.

Work it out: normally =4 + 6 = 10= and =10 ÷ 7 = 1 remainder 3=.

Multiplication works the same way; you do it normally, then take the remainder =(mod 7)=.

Division is a bit trickier.
For division to make sense, =6 ÷ 2= needs to have exactly one answer: "the number that produces 6 when multiplied by 2."

In other words, dividing by 2 is impossible if we can find any =A= and =B= where:
- =A= and =B= are different numbers
- =A × 2= and =B × 2= produce the same answer

When the modulus is 7, this is our multiplication table, with every =6= marked:

#+begin_aside
If we do clock math with a modulus of 12, this doesn't work anymore.
Consider that:
- =1 × 4 = 4 (mod 12)=
- =4 × 4 = 4 (mod 12)= (=16 ÷ 12 = 1= with a remainder of 4)

So what is =4 ÷ 4=?
It could be =1= or =4=.
#+end_aside

So, with a modulus of 7 (or /any prime number/): addition works, multiplication works, division works, and subtraction works too (check it yourself).
With these basics working, most of normal math works too.

*** Math Using Gate Sequences
Math over gate sequences turns out to be possible.
To demonstrate this, we will do math over the set of length two sequences.
These are =00=, =01=, =10=, and =11= (a finite set of 4 elements).
These are still sequences, not numbers; =10= is "one zero", not "ten".

We can define addition using the same rule we sketched earlier:

#+ATTR_HTML: :class multable
|  + | 00 | 01 | 10 | 11 |
|----+----+----+----+----|
| 00 | 00 | 01 | 10 | 11 |
| 01 | 01 | 00 | 11 | 10 |
| 10 | 10 | 11 | 00 | 01 |
| 11 | 11 | 10 | 01 | 00 |

Multiplication is trickier, so you'll have to trust me.
Here's the worked-out table, with every =01= marked so we can check that division still makes sense:

#+ATTR_HTML: :class multable hl
|  × | 00 |   01 |   10 |   11 |
|----+----+------+------+------|
| 00 | 00 |   00 |   00 |   00 |
| 01 | 00 | *01* |   10 |   11 |
| 10 | 00 |   10 |   11 | *01* |
| 11 | 00 |   11 | *01* |   10 |

You know the drill.
We can create =01= from:
- =01 × 01=
- =10 × 11= (or =11 × 10=)

You can check the rest of the values if you'd like.

So we have all four operations:
- Addition: a gate is open when exactly one of the two inputs is open
- Subtraction: exactly the same as addition (try it)
- Multiplication: complicated, but doable
- Division: complicated, but also doable

Nothing here is special about length two.
For any length $n$, multiplication can be defined on the $2^n$ bitstrings so that they form a field.
There's usually more than one way to do it, and each choice gives a different multiplication table.

#+begin_aside
You might notice that $2^n$ is not /prime/, which was our requirement from the previous section.
Galois Fields turn out to exist for any size $p^n$, where $p$ is prime and $n$ is a positive integer; the previous section was the $n=1$ case.
#+end_aside

So, yes!
We can replace numbers with sequences, redefine the operations, and have something that works like "normal math".
Multiplication of gate sequences is all we need to start producing the strange rhythmic property I've encountered.

Each element is really a polynomial, with the bits as its coefficients.
The leftmost bit is the /highest/ power (but either endian works), so =1011= is:
$$
1 x^3 + 0 x^2 + 1 x^1 + 1 x^0
$$

The modulus is an irreducible polynomial, so we do our operation normally, divide by the modulus (using long division of polynomials), and keep the remainder.

Addition and subtraction are just bitwise XOR.
Multiplying by $x$ is just a left-shift by 1.

Once we have addition, subtraction, and multiplication by $x$, we can build the "remainder function" by adding shifted copies of the irreducible polynomial until we have the remainder.

With those pieces in hand, we can implement multiplication and division of the full elements.

* Galois Orbit Sequences
My first idea was to use field elements directly to sequence drums then find some mechanism to move between sequences to evolve the produced rhythm.
An /orbit/ in a field is the path you take by starting with some field element and multiplying by some other element over and over.
We can use these to move around in the field and evolve rhythms.

In code, that'd be:
#+begin_src python :eval no
def orbit(start, gen):
    ret = []
    current = start
    while current not in ret:
        ret.append(current)
        current = current * gen   # field multiplication
    return ret
#+end_src

As long as neither =start= nor =gen= is =000=, the field is finite, so an orbit will always come back to where it started.
Some choices of =gen= visit all $2^n-1$ nonzero elements of the field; others only visit a few of them.
I'll call the first kind a /full/ orbit, since the distinction turns out to matter later.

The orbit using sequences of length 3, =gen=010=, and =start=001= is:
# modulus x^3+x^2+1; verified, do not edit these by hand
: 001  010  100  101  111  011  110

It contains seven steps, covering every element other than =000=.

** Elements as Patterns
Next we'll convert the orbit into drum sequences using each element to sequence a single drum.
We play a sequence for a while, then switch to the next sequence in the orbit.

For example, running the kick through one trip around the orbit, playing each sequence one time:

def bars(rows, top=None):
    cell, gap, bar_gap, label_w, row_gap = 26, 4, 20, 52, 10
    pad_l, pad_t, pad_b = 4, (24 if top else 6), 6
    n, nbars = len(rows[0][1][0]), len(rows[0][1])
    bar_w = n*cell + (n-1)*gap

         f'style="max-width:{w}px;width:100%;height:auto;font-family:Consolas,Monaco,monospace">']
    for bi, t in enumerate(top or []):
        x0 = pad_l + label_w + bi*(bar_w+bar_gap)
        s.append(f'<text x="{x0+bar_w/2}" y="{pad_t-9}" font-size="12" text-anchor="middle" '
                 f'fill="currentColor" opacity="0.65">{t}</text>')
    for r, (label, elements) in enumerate(rows):
        y = pad_t + r*(cell+row_gap)
        s.append(f'<text x="{pad_l}" y="{y+cell/2+5}" font-size="15" fill="currentColor">{label}</text>')

            for i, b in enumerate(el):

#+begin_aside
This is the opposite of what makes Euclidean rhythms work.
A Euclidean rhythm designs a well-spaced pattern for each instrument.
An orbit just /enumerates/ every possible pattern, and some patterns in a field aren't ones you'd pick to be musical.
#+end_aside

Let's try something different.

** Bits as Patterns
Let's "transpose" the sequences.
For sequences that are 3 elements long, we'll sequence 3 different instruments.
Then, as we move through the orbit, use each bit of each element as the gates for the 3 instruments.

The pattern an instrument plays is defined by the whole orbit, rather than a single element.

Here's a diagram to help this make sense:

def unpack(elements, labels):
    cell, gap, lab_w = 34, 6, 56
    pad_l, pad_t = 4, 14
    txt_h, brace_h, arrow_h = 18, 8, 26
    n, m = len(elements[0]), len(elements)
    pitch = cell + gap
    gy = pad_t + txt_h + brace_h + arrow_h
    w, h = pad_l + lab_w + m*pitch - gap + 4, gy + n*pitch - gap + 6
    x = lambda c: pad_l + lab_w + c*pitch
    s = [f'<svg viewBox="0 0 {w} {h}" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" '
         f'style="max-width:{w}px;width:100%;height:auto;font-family:Consolas,Monaco,monospace">']
    for c, el in enumerate(elements):
        cx = x(c) + cell/2
        s.append(f'<text x="{cx}" y="{pad_t+11}" font-size="14" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">{el}</text>')
        by = pad_t + txt_h
        s.append(f'<path d="M{x(c)+1},{by} v{brace_h-3} h{cell-2} v-{brace_h-3}" fill="none" stroke="#666" stroke-width="1.5"/>')
        ay = by + brace_h + 2
        s.append(f'<line x1="{cx}" y1="{ay}" x2="{cx}" y2="{ay+arrow_h-12}" stroke="#666" stroke-width="1.5"/>')
        s.append(f'<path d="M{cx},{ay+arrow_h-7} l-4.5,-7 l9,0 z" fill="#666"/>')
        for r, b in enumerate(el):
            on = b == "1"
            s.append(f'<rect x="{x(c)}" y="{gy+r*pitch}" width="{cell}" height="{cell}" rx="7" '
                     f'fill="{ON_COLOR if on else "none"}" stroke="{ON_COLOR if on else "#666"}" stroke-width="2"/>')
    for r, lab in enumerate(labels):
        s.append(f'<text x="{pad_l+lab_w-10}" y="{gy+r*pitch+cell/2+5}" font-size="14" '
                 f'text-anchor="end" fill="currentColor">{lab}</text>')
    s.append('</svg>')
    return "\n".join(s)

MARK = "#cc6666"

def sr(bits, k=1):
    return bits[-k:] + bits[:-k] if k else bits

def shiftchain(bits, lanes, nrows):
    cell, gap, note_w = 34, 6, 84
    pad_l, pad_t, row_gap = 8, 8, 26
    m = len(bits)
    pitch = cell + gap
    gx, gw = pad_l, m*pitch - gap
    w = gx + gw + note_w
    h = pad_t + nrows*cell + (nrows-1)*row_gap + 8
    s = [f'<svg viewBox="0 0 {w} {h}" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" '
         f'style="max-width:{w}px;width:100%;height:auto;font-family:Consolas,Monaco,monospace">']
    for r in range(nrows):
        row = sr(bits, r)
        y = pad_t + r*(cell+row_gap)
        if row in lanes:
            s.append(f'<text x="{gx+gw+16}" y="{y+cell/2+5}" font-size="14" '
                     f'fill="currentColor">{lanes[row]}</text>')
        for i, b in enumerate(row):
            on = b == "1"
            edge, sw = (MARK, 3.5) if i == r % m else ((ON_COLOR if on else "#666"), 2)
            s.append(f'<rect x="{gx+i*pitch}" y="{y}" width="{cell}" height="{cell}" rx="7" '
                     f'fill="{ON_COLOR if on else "none"}" stroke="{edge}" stroke-width="{sw}"/>')
        if r == nrows-1:
            break
        ty, cx = y + cell + row_gap/2 + 1, gx + gw/2
        s.append(f'<line x1="{cx-13}" y1="{ty-4}" x2="{cx+7}" y2="{ty-4}" '
                 f'stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.5" opacity="0.6"/>')
        s.append(f'<path d="M{cx+13},{ty-4} l-7,-4 l0,8 z" fill="currentColor" opacity="0.6"/>')
    s.append('</svg>')
    return "\n".join(s)

* This is Strange
Why would the kick's rhythm have any relationship to the snare's?

Look back at the multiplication table for the 4 element field.
Consider =01 × 01 = 01= and =11 × 11 = 10=.
The inputs to both multiplications end in a =1=, but the results =01= and =10= end in =1= and =0= respectively.
Clearly multiplication "mixes" all the bits up somehow.

If multiplication mixes up the entire sequence, how do we end up with the /same/ sequence shifted around when we turn the sequences sideways?

Weirder still, the orbit visits every element of the field (other than =000=), so the only thing it decides is the order they come in.
An orbit is also a loop, so starting it somewhere else is the same trip around; what matters is the cycle.
There are 720 cycles through the 7 elements, and only 48 of them have the shifting property.

Write the orbit as $e(t) = start \times gen^t$.

Suppose some row repeated early, so that reading it from step $t$ gave the same sequence as reading it from step $t+j$.
Addition is XOR, so that's the same as saying the row of $e(t) + e(t+j)$ is zero the whole way along.

But adding two orbits together gives another orbit:
$$e(t) + e(t+j) = start \times gen^t + start \times gen^{t+j} = \left(start \times (1 + gen^j)\right) \times gen^t$$

Short of a full lap, $gen^j$ is never 1, so $1 + gen^j$ isn't zero, and neither is that new starting element.
That makes it a real orbit, and a real orbit can't have an all-zero row: it visits every nonzero element, including ones with a 1 in that position.

So no row can repeat early, and all $2^n-1$ shifts of a row are different sequences.
The modulus forces every row to obey the same rule, where each step is the XOR of a fixed few steps before it, and there are exactly $2^n-1$ nonzero sequences that obey it.
The shifts account for all of them, so every row has to be one of the shifts.

*** Where this breaks

The proof uses the orbit being /full/ in three places.

"Short of a full lap, $gen^j$ is never 1" needs $gen$ to have order exactly $2^n-1$.
"A real orbit can't have an all-zero row" needs the orbit to visit every nonzero element.
The count at the end needs the row to be $2^n-1$ steps long, so the shifts cover every nonzero solution instead of just some of them.

Without that, it can fail.
Here's a 5 step orbit in $GF(16)$, modulus =10011=, =gen=1000=, =start=0001=:

: orbit  0001 1000 1100 1010 1111
: rows   01111
:        00101
:        00011
:        10001

No row is all zeros, but these still aren't rotations of each other.
Rotating a sequence can't change how many hits it has, and the first row has four while the rest have two.

A full orbit hits every nonzero element once, so every row ends up with the same number of hits ($2^{n-1}$).
A short orbit only gets part of the field, and the rows come out uneven.
An all-zero row is the extreme case.

Plenty of short orbits still work out to rotations, they just aren't guaranteed to.
I haven't worked out which ones.
Sorting them by length turns up something suggestive though.
In $GF(64)$, every orbit of length 3 or 7 works, no orbit of length 21 does, and orbits of length 9 work exactly a ninth of the time.
3 and 7 are $2^2-1$ and $2^3-1$, the orbit lengths of the $GF(4)$ and $GF(8)$ subfields sitting inside $GF(64)$, so those orbits have a smaller field to be full /in/.
The counterexample above has length 5, and $GF(16)$ has no subfield to match it.

That covers the lengths that always work and the ones that never do, but not the length 9 orbits that sometimes do.

* Conclusion
It turns out I hadn't discovered anything.
Reading an orbit a row at a time is a [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear-feedback_shift_register][Linear Feedback Shift Register]]: a pile of bits that shifts along on every tick, with a new one XORed in at the end.
They've been used since the 1960s to make random-looking bits in hardware, the sequences they produce are called /m-sequences/, and "every bit of the register runs the same sequence at a different offset" is a standard fact about them.

So I rebuilt a piece of old hardware by accident, and it happens to make decent drum patterns.

The rhythms work.
I'm still looking for the melodies.
